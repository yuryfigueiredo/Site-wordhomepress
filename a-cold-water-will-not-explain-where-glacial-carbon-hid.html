Cold Water Will Not Explain Where Glacial Carbon Hid

Ice-age CO2 lows are usually taught as a story about cold water soaking up more gas. A sealed surface is the stronger candidate, and the argument between them is a measurement problem.

BY Dr. Aris Thorne • Nov 18, 2026 • 7 MIN READ

Cold Water Is the Cheapest Calculation

The standard retelling of the ice ages runs like this. Water gets cold, cold water sinks, cold water holds more gas, and the atmosphere empties. It is tidy, it is taught, and it does not carry the weight placed on it. Solubility is a real effect and the cheapest one to calculate, which is why it keeps winning introductions.

The claim argued here: during a glacial, the ocean's appetite for carbon was set by whether its surface was sealed from the air. Colder water helps, and biology helps, but both need an opening before any carbon can be trapped. Close the surface and the pumps below stop mattering. Temperature sets how much the water can carry.

Carbon dioxide dissolves more readily in cold water, and the deep ocean is cold everywhere already. During a glacial it was colder still, and that extra dissolution is a genuine contribution. The problem is where that water has to go. The deep sea never breathes; it inherits whatever the surface agreed to hand down.

The Surface Sets the Terms

Gas crosses the air-sea boundary in the top millimeters, and it crosses quickly. Everything below is a queue, and carbon waits for the water that touched sky to sink and return. Change the queue and you change the inventory without touching the chemistry. That is the line between a solubility argument and a ventilation argument.

Old water is rich water, and for a plain reason. Dead particles fall from the sunlit layer into the dark, and the bacteria that eat them leave carbon behind. A parcel that surfaces often accumulates less than one that surfaces rarely. Time is the variable. Cold water describes the capacity, never the amount.

If the deep basins were older during a glacial, the drawdown happens without new physics being invented. The ocean stops ventilating part of itself, and the falling carbon stays fallen. That is a lid under another name. It is also a claim about circulation, which is harder to find in mud than in a formula.

The Deep Ocean Opens in the South

On the map we have, most deep water reaches the air in one neighborhood rather than in all of them. Around Antarctica, layered water rises, breathes with the atmosphere, then plunges again on its way north. Seal that ring and the interior stops trading with the air. This is why the glacial question keeps returning to cold southern water.

Sea ice is the obvious lid. It blocks gas across the water it covers. It leaves brine behind, and that brine is salty enough to stop the water beneath it from rising. Stratification finishes the job: a fresh, light skin floating on heavy water is a lid nobody has to model.

A cold glass of something fizzy keeps bubbles clinging to its wall long after the top has gone flat. Gas leaves the exposed face first, and the trapped pockets give slowly. Scale that behavior to a continent and you have the shape of every lid argument in the field. Ice does most of the waiting.

The Best Case for Cold Water

Give the temperature story its strongest form, because it has not been refuted. Dissolution is measured in laboratories. Ancient sea ice is inferred from a handful of shells. A model that cools the whole surface and changes nothing else still moves a respectable amount of carbon downward. That cooling reached every basin, not the southern ones alone.

There is a dust argument too, and it belongs to biology rather than thermodynamics. Glacial winds carried more grit to sea, and that grit is plausibly rich in the nutrient that limits southern plant growth. More plants mean more falling debris, and more debris means carbon arriving in the dark before it can return. Cold water would still have to hold what the plants deliver.

The honest weakness of the lid story is that it needs a sealed surface and a steady supply of carbon to fill it. A lid with no supply leaves the deep water empty. An empty deep ocean is not a low-carbon atmosphere. Fertilization offers the supply; ice offers the seal. Neither is complete alone, and the pair is the current honest position.

The Counterweight Nobody Mentions

A biological explanation has a built-in leak. Plants that grow at the surface build shells and skeletons out of carbon that will not be buried. When those structures dissolve on the way down, the carbon comes back, and it returns as gas rather than as fuel. That leak is chemistry, not opinion.

This is the counterpump. It is why a heavier rain of debris does not automatically mean a thinner atmosphere. What decides the outcome is burial: carbon in sediment is gone, carbon still dissolved is only parked. Burial is a boring, decisive process. Most glacial arguments are quietly arguments about where the mud ended up. Carbonate chemistry is where a good argument goes to look modest.

What the Records Can and Cannot Say

Now the uncomfortable part, which is where a new study earns trust or a headline. Each proxy family sees one face of the problem. Ice measures the atmosphere; mud measures the water. Neither is a direct read of the surface. A temperature from a shell and an ice estimate from an algal residue are different kinds of evidence.

Ventilation age is the measurement that would actually separate a lid from cold. It asks how long a water mass has gone without touching the air. Reconstructing it across a glacial boundary is notoriously hard. Boron and cadmium isotope work gestures at carbonate chemistry, and the gas records stay silent about the interior.

Both effects are real.

Co-registration is the underused part of the method. Proxies drawn from the same centimeter of one core answer each other. Proxies from distant sites argue forever. Much of the disagreement outlives the data because nobody drilled those sites together. A single well-dated site beats a survey of vague ones.

The Present Tense of an Old Ocean

None of this belongs to historians alone. We are adding carbon to a surface ocean that is warmer and more stratified than the glacial water described here. The lid argument predicts a weakening sink, because stratification is a lid and we are assembling one. The cold-water argument predicts the same drift, more slowly, and for a different reason.

Both stories agree about the next few years and disagree about the century. That is the useful kind of disagreement, because it points at a measurement rather than a mood. A sink that weakens still works. The gap between weakening and stopping is where decisions get made, and nobody has measured that gap.

What This Column Cannot Say

The specific numbers are the part a reader should take from the paper rather than from this column. This piece was built from a headline and a link, which is a thin basis for any quantitative statement. How much more carbon, how fast, and which basin led are precisely the questions the study answers. That is a limit of sourcing rather than of the mechanism.

What survives the missing numbers is the order of the mechanisms, and that order is arguable today. A sealed surface outranks a colder one, because cold water has to be somewhere before it can hold anything. That ranking is a reading of the physics rather than a discovery. It fails if deep ventilation turns out to have been unchanged.

We could not confirm which mechanism the new work privileges, or whether it privileges any of them. A glacial ocean and a warming one may be telling one story in opposite directions. Read the paper for its numbers. Read this column for the shape of the disagreement, and hold it only as far as the sourcing reaches.
