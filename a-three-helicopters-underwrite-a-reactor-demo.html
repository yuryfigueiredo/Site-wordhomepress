Three Helicopters Underwrite NASA's Reactor Demonstration

SkyFall flies on a nuclear carrier that may not be ready, and its own project scientist says a Falcon 9 would do.

BY Claire Sterling • Nov 21, 2026 • 6 MIN READ

Six people in March, two hundred by November

NASA kick-started SkyFall in March 2026 with roughly six people. Nine months later, more than 200 were working on it. Matthew Golombek, the project scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, gave that figure on November 10, 2026, at a virtual meeting of the Mars Exploration Program Analysis Group. He called the growth incredibly rapid. Helicopter programs do not multiply that fast because a science question suddenly demanded them.

The fleet is three Ingenuity-class helicopters, and their ride to Mars is a reactor. Space Reactor-1 Freedom is described as NASA's first fission-powered interplanetary spacecraft, with a launch planned for late 2028. The pairing is sold as engineering convenience. It reads closer to program management: a reactor demonstrator needs a payload a budget office can picture. Three small helicopters above Mars are that kind of payload.

Nothing in the profile requires a reactor

The flight plan is a long one. SR-1 Freedom launches in late 2028, cruises, and makes an initial Mars flyby in 2029. A second approach, in the fall of 2030, releases an entry capsule carrying the helicopters. Those dates come from the Space.com report by Leonard David, published on November 18, 2026.

Chemical stages have delivered Mars spacecraft for decades, and Golombek put the alternative plainly. If SR-1 is not ready, he said, SkyFall could launch on any conventional vehicle, and something like a Falcon 9 would be more than sufficient. The mission is the helicopters. The reactor is the part that has to earn its place on the manifest.

Scott Hubbard's version of the same point is blunter. Nuclear electric propulsion, he argued, saves no time on a Mars transit. Its value in his reading sits elsewhere: risk reduction, lunar use, the outer planets, and longer shots such as interstellar probes. A helicopter drop at Mars is not where that argument wins on its own.

The reversed sequence

Hubbard ran NASA's Ames Research Center and served as the agency's first Mars Program Director at headquarters. He is now a retired adjunct professor of aeronautics and astronautics at Stanford. His objection to SkyFall is procedural rather than technical, and it is the strongest thing in the report. In the ordinary order, a science requirement produces a mission, and the mission then buys hardware. Here the hardware arrives first, and the science community, in his words, scrambles backward into requirements that match what the machine can do.

He calls the result an unusual set of management issues, and notes that the Planetary Decadal objectives are broad enough to absorb the move. A survey written to cover many questions can be read as authorizing one specific machine, and nobody can argue the document out of that reading. Broad is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Hubbard expects the community to cope anyway. He has heard the complaints about a decision already made, and he does not predict a revolt.

What the helicopters would measure

Strip the politics and the instrument story is short. Three helicopters carry identical payloads of five instruments plus engineering navigation cameras, and spin up about a kilometer above the surface. Each lands on its own, then flies every other solar day. A sol runs 24 hours, 39 minutes and 35 seconds, so the fleet works on a clock no driver on Earth would recognize.

The star instrument is a ground-penetrating radar with a flexible antenna small enough to fly. It hunts buried rocks, shallow layering, and ice. Golombek frames the mission as a resolution gap. Orbiters see a planet, landers see a few meters around themselves, and the middle scale is empty. That middle scale is where a wheel path gets decided.

Range is the multiplier. From one landing point the vehicles could travel tens to hundreds of kilometers. The prime mission is six months, with a one-year goal, and arrival is timed for northern spring, when dust storms are less likely to ground the fleet. Those operating numbers do more for the science case than the propulsion choice does.

The strongest case for the pairing

The counter-argument deserves its best version. Ice is the prize, and a carrier that already holds a 2030 arrival reaches it before a helicopter mission still hunting its own window. That comparison is ours, not NASA's. What the agency says is narrower and better documented: aerial scouting tells a later rover where to go.

Golombek's own example is a crater the mission digs for itself. Discarded descent hardware, fuel tanks and cruise-stage pieces, is heavy enough to survive entry and hit hard. If ice sits centimeters below, he said, the helicopters could reach their own fresh icy craters before the exposed ice sublimates. That is a measurement no orbiter can schedule.

Then there is terrain. These are described as the smallest landers ever attempted on Mars. That raises the site bar: virtually no rocks, low slopes, no large craters, and a surface not overloaded with dust. Arcadia Planitia in the northern lowlands clears that list and sits on regional near-surface ice. Golombek notes that JPL once flagged the same plain as good Starship terrain, in workshops SpaceX sponsored.

What nobody has scheduled

Two things are missing from the record. No science traverse has been plotted for the helicopters, and no readiness date exists for the reactor. Hubbard's own hedge covers the second gap: SR-1 Freedom is a technology demo that may or may not be ready for the 2028 Mars window. The first gap is quieter, and bigger.

Neither gap is a physics problem.

The planetary community gets two workshops next year to review SkyFall science and narrow landing sites, with the final call made at NASA headquarters. Until those produce a traverse plan, the six-month mission is defined by what the aircraft can carry rather than by a question only helicopters can answer.

What to watch instead

The practical consequence is a reading instruction. Judge SkyFall by the instrument list and the traverse plan that comes out of next year's workshops, not by the propulsion badge on the carrier. If a Falcon 9 is genuinely sufficient, as Golombek says, the helicopters' science survives a reactor slipping. The schedule risk then sits on the side that needed the ride.

Two limits belong in the open. Hubbard's judgment that SkyFall was approved for programmatic or political reasons arrives with no decision memo attached. The report gives one NASA answer on the coupling: the agency considers the two endeavors a pair. It gives no cost comparison between the reactor ride and the conventional one. The staffing curve is the hardest number in the report, roughly six people in March and more than 200 in November.

The page carries one more tell. Its line under the headline is a single quotation, and the byline reads Space Insider Columnist, which is a column rather than a news report. That is worth knowing before anyone treats the pairing as settled engineering.
