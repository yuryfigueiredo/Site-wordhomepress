The Discounts on One Black Friday Page All Add Up

Seventy-eight product cards reconcile their percentages to the point. Eight of them describe a price the reader should not pay.

BY Devon Brooks • Nov 26, 2026 • 6 MIN READ

The page and its fifteen days

The Verge opened its early Black Friday guide on November 11 and last revised it on November 26, at 4:55 PM UTC. The holiday it serves is November 27. Between those dates the page gathered 79 product cards, 129 purchase buttons, and a printed discount percentage on 78 of them.

The arithmetic on this page is sound. That is the finding, and it is also the boundary of the comfort it offers. Every percentage the page prints reconciles with the two numbers sitting beside it. On eight cards, that percentage describes a button the reader should not click.

What the buttons actually offer

Count the cards by how many retailers each one hands over, and the distribution is lopsided. Forty-five cards carry two or more purchase buttons. On 32 of them every button shows the identical figure, so the printed percentage means the same thing wherever the reader lands.

The Apple AirTag, now in its second generation, is $18 at Amazon, Walmart and Best Buy alike. Across the whole page the visible buttons run from $8, for a TP-Link smart plug, up to $1,000 for a Dreame robot vacuum.

Some cards hold more than one number because the product itself is a plan. The Disney Plus and Hulu bundle prints $4.99 for the ad-supported month and $14.99 for the ad-free one. Both are described as a deal, and which one reads well depends on a choice the discount did not make for you.

On the other 13 cards the buttons disagree. The widest gap sits on the Roborock Qrevo Curv 2 Flow, listed at $550 at Amazon and $600 at Best Buy and at Roborock's own store. The AirPods Pro 3 card runs $149 at Amazon and Walmart against $180 at Best Buy.

The arithmetic holds

The reflexive reading of a deals page is that its numbers are decoration. That reading fails here. For all 78 cards carrying both a struck reference price and a percentage, the percentage equals one minus the current price divided by that reference, within ordinary rounding.

Every one reconciled.

The single card that declines the format is a Lego bonsai set listed at $65 with no struck price and no claim attached. It asks nothing of the reader, which makes it the calmest object on the page.

Where the percentage stops belonging to the button

A percentage printed above three buttons is a claim about one of them. Take the AirPods Pro 3. The card shows $149 against a struck $249 and announces 40% off, and that subtraction is correct. The third button reads $180. At $180 the same reference yields 27.7%, so one card supports two discount claims and marks neither.

The shape repeats with less money on the table. The Iniu P55-E2 power bank carries a struck $37 and a printed 43% off on a $21 Amazon button, while its Walmart button reads $28. That is 24.3% off the identical reference. Eighteen percentage points separate two retailers on a $37 object.

Run the test across all 45 multi-button cards and eight of them move the real discount by at least 5 percentage points between their cheapest and dearest buttons. Three of those eight sit in the headphones and earbuds group, the category the page leads with.

The strongest case for the page

Nobody is being cheated here. The cheapest button is real, purchasable, and dated to the hour. The guide states plainly that prices are rising across the board and that a good discount is not proof of a historic low. It also warns that the list will keep changing as retailers move.

Its transparency goes further than most commerce pages. A disclosure line sits under the byline block, saying the site may earn a commission from a link. The gear is described as tried and tested rather than merely cheap.

The filter row above the listings is the most honest object on the page. Under $25, then $26 to $50, $51 to $150, $151 to $400, and $401 and up. It sorts by money rather than by enthusiasm, and it asks the reader to declare a budget before any card gets to argue. The bands are written in whole dollars, so a $25.50 item belongs to none of them.

What no card can carry

What cannot be learned from this page is the question readers arrive with, which is whether $149 is a low price for those earbuds. The page publishes no price history. The reference price arrives without provenance: no date, no named retailer, no statement of who sets the struck number.

The revision trail proves the point better than any complaint. The latest update entry, dated November 26, announces added deals for the Apple Watch SE 3 and the Insta360 Link 2 webcam. Cards arrive on this page between one reading and the next.

This account carries the same limit it applies to the page. Every figure above was read from the source as it stood on November 26, from its structured offer data and its rendered cards. No cart was opened, no tax was added, and a price read at one hour belongs to a page the publisher describes as continuously updated.

What to do before Friday

Treat each card as a small table rather than a verdict. Read the printed percentage as a property of the cheapest button. Then divide the price on the button you would actually use by the struck number the card supplies, and compare the two results. A discount is two prices and a subtraction.

On most of this page the two answers agree, and 32 cards never raise the question at all. On eight of them they do not, and those eight are where a buyer planning around a headline percentage loses the most. The work the page will not do takes one division per card.
