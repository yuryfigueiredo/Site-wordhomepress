Black Friday Smart Home Deals Measure Stock, Not Software

A deal roundup tracks what retailers discount on the day. Whether the hardware still works once its vendor moves on is a different document, and a more expensive one.

BY Devon Brooks • Nov 27, 2026 • 8 MIN READ

The Verge published its roundup of Black Friday smart home deals on November 27, 2026. The page exists, it is dated, and it is the only fact about this year's discounts that this piece can stand behind.

The argument here is narrow and unfashionable. A deals page is a document about inventory. It ranks what retailers can move in volume over a weekend, and stays silent on whether the device will still take firmware at all.

The list measures the discount and stops there

Every deal roundup runs on the same arithmetic. A device carries one number at full price and a smaller number today, and the gap between them becomes the order of the list. That arithmetic is honest work. It measures the discount, which is the part everyone can agree on, and it stops there.

Smart home hardware differs from most consumer electronics in one structural way: it arrives attached to an account and a service the buyer cannot own outright. A lock and a light bridge keep roughly the value they had the day they ship. What changes is whether the company behind them keeps writing software. A discount cannot compensate for that second variable, and a deals page has no column for it.

The strongest case for the roundup

Someone has to do the tedious work. Prices across retailers change by the hour on the day, stock disappears, and a page that refreshes through the afternoon saves the reader that scanning. The reporting behind a good roundup is genuine and fast, and it deserves credit for what it can see.

That claim establishes the page as a scanner. Judging is a separate job, and a roundup does not pretend to do it. It answers a narrower question: which devices are cheaper now than they were yesterday. The questions that decide whether a connected device is worth owning sit outside that sentence. One is how long the software is maintained. The other is what the gadget does when the vendor's cloud breaks.

Firmware is the schedule nobody prints

A connected device has a hardware life and a software life. The silicon lasts; the support arrangement does not. Manufacturers stop signing updates, the app moves on, and a working motor behind a dead login stops being a device. Nothing in a discount table carries the date of that event, and the table is not trying to.

Publishers do not print a support window because vendors rarely commit to one in writing. Stopping updates on a product already sold is generally not a refundable event, which explains why the date is missing rather than buried. The closest a buyer gets to a warranty on software is the vendor's record with its own older models. That record lives in owner forums and in teardowns, two places the listing does not reach.

The offline test decides more than the price

Ask what a device does with no connection. A switch that toggles its relay locally keeps working when the internet fails. A switch that needs a trip to a server first is a service with a button on it. That distinction costs nothing to check and changes the value of every discount in the category.

Deal pages rarely state a local control path. The answer lives in a teardown or a printed manual, and the protocol names are on the box. A device that talks only to its vendor's app has already said what it is.

This takes a minute.

Categories age at different speeds, and the offline question sorts them better than price does. A camera that records locally keeps most of its function when a subscription disappears; a camera whose footage lives only in a vendor's cloud loses its purpose. A vacuum that maps its runs on the device keeps cleaning after the app is abandoned. One whose maps live on a server inherits a shorter life than its brushes.

Who sets the number next to the slash

A discount is a ratio, and the denominator usually comes from the seller. The reference price sits in a listing rather than a receipt. Listings also drift, since models are renamed and bundles are taken apart. The type helps: the reference is often printed smaller and grayer than the offer, which makes the gap read wider than the arithmetic justifies. A discount can be real and misleading in the same hour.

Commerce pages of this kind generally carry affiliate links, which is a business model rather than a scandal. It is a reason to ask who supplied the baseline, and to check a second price source before treating a percentage as fact. A retailer that sets both the reference price and the offer has no obligation to record the older number carefully.

Lighting ages well, speakers age badly

Lighting ages well. A bulb that dims on a radio command still dims when its maker forgets the model number. The function is small and the protocol is old. Switches behave the same way, and a wired relay in a wall is the closest this category gets to a durable good.

Speakers age worst, because almost everything they do is rented. A smart speaker is a microphone attached to a service. When that service retires an assistant, the hardware loses most of what it was bought for. Locks and doorbells sit in between, with a complication. A security product abandoned by its vendor is worse than useless, because the door still closes and stops reporting.

Five questions that beat any ranking

A buyer can do better than trusting the order of a list. The questions below take a few minutes and apply across the connected house, whatever the page says is on offer. They also produce a shortlist that holds after the discount expires, which is the only test a purchase has to pass.

  • Does the vendor publish a support or security update window for this exact model? Look for a dated commitment on the product page, not a support article that promises updates in general.
  • Does the device keep working with the network disconnected, using the protocol printed on the box? Pull the router plug for an afternoon and walk through the automations you actually run.
  • Which company operates the cloud service the device depends on, and has it retired a product line before? A vendor that has killed one line will handle this one the same way when the unit economics turn.
  • Is the reference price documented anywhere besides the page showing the discount? An old receipt or an archived listing carrying the same figure turns a ratio into a fact.
  • What happens to the warranty if the account is closed or the service is sold? That answer usually sits in the terms of service, and its absence is itself information.

These questions have public answers more often than buyers expect, and a deal that survives all five is worth taking whatever rank it held. One question does more work than the rest: the second. Everything a device will do over the following years depends on whether it can act on its own.

What a test would have to measure

A desk that intends a verdict rather than a list has to run the same hours on each device. Nothing here was measured, and that is stated before the method instead of hidden in a footnote. The criteria below describe what a test of discounted gear would need.

  • Pairing and re-pairing after a factory reset, timed twice. A device that is slow to rejoin a network charges that delay back to the owner on every migration.
  • Behavior with the internet cut at the router for a full day, automations included. This is the run that shows which functions were ever local and which were borrowed.
  • Latency between the physical button and the light that changes. Time it on video, standing where the switch actually lives rather than beside the hub.
  • Whether an update altered a working routine, and how the vendor announced it. Silence in the changelog is the usual answer, and it is why owners keep screenshots of what used to work.

Those measurements are slow and unglamorous, and they are the difference between a review and a price alert. A deals page is not built to run them, since its deadline is the weekend. A lab cycle takes longer than a sale, and that difference explains most of the disagreement between the two kinds of page.

What this desk could not confirm

This piece states no prices. That is a limit of the reporting, stated plainly. Every figure in a deals roundup needs a verification timestamp, and none could be confirmed from here. The source page carries a date of November 27, 2026, which names the day and not the hour. Anyone who needs a number should read it off the storefront that will honor it.

Which models are discounted, at what price, against what baseline: the source page names them. This copy could not verify a single one at a stated hour, and a number borrowed from last year would be worse than none. The gap between a dated page and a live cart is where the reader has to work.

The practical consequence is a change of sequence. The roundup goes first because it is fast, and the five questions go second because they decide whether a discount means anything. A buyer who reverses that order keeps the device long after the discount is forgotten.
