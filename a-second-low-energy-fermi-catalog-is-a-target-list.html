Fermi's Low-energy Catalog Turns a Detection Gap Into a Target List

The Second Catalog of Low-energy Fermi-LAT Sources reports 450 sources between 20 and 200 MeV, and the 73 that match nothing are the part worth arguing about.

BY Dr. Aris Thorne • Nov 22, 2026 • 6 MIN READ

The document, and the number inside it

The paper has 133 authors, 89 references and an article number: 40 in volume 287 of The Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series. It appeared on 12 November 2026. Its title is deliberately dull, The Second Catalog of Low-energy Fermi-LAT Sources, which leaves no room for a press release. The abbreviation is 2FLE.

Read the catalog as a target list rather than a map, and one figure separates it from a routine incremental release. The Fermi Large Area Telescope reports 450 sources between 20 and 200 megaelectronvolts. Those were cross-checked against radio, optical and X-ray data, and 377 came back with a known counterpart. The remaining 73 sit in a band where nobody had a decent list before, and 60 of them look previously unknown. My position is that the 73 are the result, and the 450 are the instrument reporting on itself.

Both ends of the spectrum already had instruments

Nothing about the low end was unobserved. Chandra covers the X-ray side, and the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope covers the top of the gamma range, where a track can be reconstructed. What sat between them was a band with far fewer usable detections. The name astronomers use for it, the MeV gap, treats an equipment problem as a fact about the universe.

Why this part of the spectrum hides

Gamma rays do not reflect. A mirror that works for visible light, or for X-rays at grazing incidence, does nothing useful at megaelectronvolt energies. So the instrument is closer to a particle detector in orbit than to a telescope. It records where a photon converted inside layers of tungsten and silicon, then infers direction from the debris. There is no lens.

Above roughly a gigaelectronvolt, the conversion does enough damage that a line can be traced back to a source. In the 20 to 200 MeV band the photon scatters more loosely. The report gives the consequence plainly. A single 20 MeV photon localizes to a patch of sky about 20 degrees across, more than 40 times the width of the full moon. Add background from the Sun and from the rest of the galaxy, and one event is nearly useless.

Fourteen years of the same detector

The trick was not a better detector. It was exposure. The telescope launched in 2008 and has run long enough that 14 years of faint, badly localized events can be pooled and modeled statistically. Time did the work. That is what turns a 20-degree smear into a position small enough to argue with a catalogue entry.

The population splits cleanly. The catalog puts 325 sources outside the Milky Way and 52 inside it. Those two numbers add to 377, exactly the count with counterparts, and the agreement is the first sign the classification is not arbitrary. Almost half the sample is flat-spectrum radio quasars. These are blazars, supermassive black holes with a plasma jet aimed at Earth, and the report says they put most of their gamma-ray energy into this band.

The 52 galactic entries are mostly pulsars, the spinning leftovers a star leaves when it explodes. One is a globular cluster, M2, which the paper flags as a possible first detection at gamma-ray energies. A single cluster is not a population, and one more entry does not change that. It is the kind of object a follow-up programme points at first, not a statistical result.

The part of the catalog nobody can match

Now the interesting number. Seventy-three of the 450 have no clear counterpart in radio, optical or X-ray data. Sixty of those look like previously unknown gamma-ray sources. The report is careful that some may be two neighbouring objects smeared into one detection, and that caution is the paper's own, not a journalist's hedge.

One entry deserves more attention than the rest have earned. Its designation is 2FLE J0340.8+5258, coordinates wearing a serial number, which is what a position looks like before anything has been matched to it. The report says it also appears in data from an observatory in China, and that it produces radiation across nine orders of magnitude. A source that loud and that unmatched is a problem for the matching procedure before it is a problem for astrophysics.

That is the result worth reading.

The case for reading it as noise

The strongest argument against the 73 is that a statistical catalog promotes its own residuals into entries. Pooling 14 years of one detector's data averages down random error and leaves systematic error untouched. If the model of the diffuse background is slightly wrong, the residuals are sources as far as the fitting is concerned. A pulsar magnetosphere that the simulation puts in the wrong place does the same damage. The fit cannot tell.

There is a second version of the same worry. A sample dominated by blazars may describe who crosses the detection threshold in this band rather than who emits there. Flat-spectrum radio quasars are bright, variable and already known, so 2FLE may be a census of the loudest rather than the most common. The paper does not settle that, and more exposure on the same instrument would not settle it.

What is still missing

The instrument has done what it can do. The report says Fermi-LAT appears to have reached its limit with this data set. That is a strange sentence for a 14-year survey to write, and a useful one for a reader to believe. Nothing here will squeeze a fourth significant digit out of the same tungsten and silicon.

The next band down is being instrumented. NASA's Compton Spectrometer and Imager, COSI, is built for 0.2 to 5 MeV. The report says it depends on a germanium detector that only became practical in the last few years. Read next to 2FLE, that gives the unknown a shape. Fermi has pushed localization about as far as a pair-conversion detector can go, and the band below it needs a different way of catching photons.

How to use what is on the table

For anyone with time to spend, 2FLE is a worklist. Start with the 60 that appear to be genuinely new, then work the rest of the 73. Check whether a position matches a known variable first, because a blazar caught in a low state looks like nothing. That is a concrete programme, and it does not need a new instrument to begin.

The paper is a refereed supplement with a registered DOI, which means the tables exist somewhere a critic can go and disagree. The count that reached the news came through a secondary report, and this piece sits one layer further out still. Anyone holding the tables can do better than a summary of them.

The limit is worth stating as plainly as the result. This piece rests on one reported catalog and one journal record. The source tables were not reanalysed here, and none of the 73 positions was checked against a simulator. Anyone who reads the tables line by line may find the number smaller. What the 73 turn out to be is not in the catalog.
