A Lunar Land Office Has to Open Before the Claims Do

Space.com's frontier warning reads better as an argument about survey data, reference frames and who gets to file first.

BY Dr. Aris Thorne • Nov 21, 2026 • 6 MIN READ

What the frontier phrase is for

"Wild West" summons a shootout, a saloon and a sheriff who rides in late. A real lunar dispute would be duller and harder to see coming: two filings, one crater, two incompatible coordinate systems. Space.com ran the scientists' warning on Nov 20, 2026, and the frontier phrase is doing most of the work in the telling.

My reading: the danger is administrative, and administrative failure is far cheaper to prevent than to litigate. A lunar claim becomes real the moment somebody publishes coordinates and nobody is positioned to contest them. Underneath the frontier language sits one dull demand: a register that exists before the filings and can say no.

The analogy flatters us

Read the borrowed phrase closely and it undersells the problem. The historical West that the warning reaches for was thick with government: surveyors, land offices, courts riding circuits, a national currency behind all of it. Those institutions were frequently wrong and often cruel. They were still institutions, and they still failed to stop the worst of what happened.

The Moon has none of that. There is no survey, no registry, no court standing between claimants. Whoever writes the first workable rules will write them over terrain nobody has mapped to a shared datum, without precedent of any kind.

Nobody has ever fined a rover.

How a lunar dispute would actually run

Take the boring version. Two operators publish polygons over the same crater, each in its own reference frame, each with its own publication date. Neither filing is dishonest. Both describe a real place, and the descriptions fail to overlap in a way a third party can check. That is the emergency: two honest maps and no office that can reconcile them.

Damage follows from proximity, rarely from intent. A lander's exhaust moves regolith. Regolith travels farther across an airless body than anyone raised on wind and water would guess. A site worth a century of samples can be spoiled by a neighbor who never meant to arrive. Afterward there is nobody with standing to hear the complaint.

The remedy sounds trivial until you trace what it prevents. Name the parcel in words anyone can read. Fix the coordinates in one stated reference frame, and publish the date they were fixed. Decide who arbitrates two names over one crater, and decide while the answer is still cheap. Each of those is a filing-cabinet measure, cheap in advance and priceless afterwards. Nobody has built the drawer yet.

Enclosure arrives before the digging

The pressure rarely begins with extraction. It begins with traffic. Somebody needs an uninterrupted view of one patch of sky, and the neighbor's landing pad sits inside it. Debris has to come down somewhere too, and that somewhere is chosen by whoever is flying. Each of those is a claim on a location, filed as an engineering constraint, which makes it look unarguable.

This is why the warning reaches past mining, and why it can be answered cheaply. A protected site needs a published exclusion list, updated on a schedule, in the same coordinate frame the operators already use. The rest of it is press-release work.

The strongest case against the warning

The best objection comes from anyone who has read frontier history closely. Land offices were never neutral machinery. A registry is run by somebody, in some language, on somebody's datum. The parties that set those conventions early hold an advantage no later agreement can tax away. A registration regime written by the few and imposed on the many works as a fence, and a fence around what nobody owns is its own injury. Anyone who has watched a claim club price out the people already living there has good grounds for distrust. Order, delivered late and from outside, is just the winner's paperwork.

This objection is real, and it should cost the warning something. What it does not buy is permission to improvise. Rules get written either way. The set written during a live dispute will be drafted by whoever already has hardware on the surface, while everyone else learns the terms from a press release. Filing systems are slow and unfair in advance. The alternative is faster, and much less fair afterwards.

What this piece cannot claim

The honest limit: this desk had one source line and no article text. Space.com's report is dated Nov 20, 2026, and attributes the concern to scientists. Which scientists, in what capacity, asking for which instrument, I cannot say, and I have not guessed. A press statement and a policy differ precisely at those details.

Nothing here should be read as a count. No number of signatories survived the trip to this desk, and no quantity of accessible ice, nor any estimate of what a registry would cost. Each of those figures is where a real argument turns. An invented one would be decoration. That is why this section is short and the last section is checkable.

The clauses worth reading first

Read any lunar governance proposal for its boring clauses. Four of them carry everything else.

  • Who registers a claim, on what form, funded by what fee? A register without a budget is a mailing list.
  • Which reference frame, and which epoch, do the coordinates use? Without both, two honest operators can describe the same boulder differently.
  • Who hears a dispute between parties whose home states disagree, and what can that body actually order?
  • What happens to a site after its operator leaves? A claim that dies with the hardware records occupancy, and nothing more.

Those four are checkable in a single sitting and almost never appear in a summary. You will find them in a technical annex, in smaller type than the promises, beside resolution figures and date stamps. A proposal that names an adjudicator and a datum already belongs to a different genre from one that commends best practices at the landing site.

What a warning like this can buy

Used well, a warning moves one line item. It buys survey work: tracking stations, shared imagery at a stated resolution, one coordinate convention everybody signs onto, a queue where landing sites are published before they are occupied. That work is unglamorous, it is cheap beside a mission, and it is the one part of the argument nobody can redo later.

So the practical test is small enough to run yourself. Next time a lunar governance story crosses your desk, skip the adjectives and find the reference frame in the technical annex. If the coordinates are defined and dated, somebody has already begun governing. If they are not, nothing else in the document is binding yet.
