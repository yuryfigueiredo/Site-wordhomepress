A Universe 26.7 Billion Years Old Passes Three Tests and Skips the Deciding One

A consistency paper drops dark matter and dark energy, then survives nucleosynthesis, 175 rotation curves and cosmic chronometers. The microwave background is still a promise.

BY Dr. Aris Thorne • Nov 7, 2026 • 6 MIN READ

The document at the center of this story carries a DOI that reads 10.3390/universe12070193. It appeared in Universe, an MDPI journal, in 2026, and lists Rajendra P. Gupta first. The title describes a consistency test rather than a victory. Gupta is an adjunct professor in the Department of Physics at the University of Ottawa. The report names Dr. Nikolaos Samaras, a postdoctoral researcher, alongside him.

Read past the headline number and the argument changes shape. Three independent checks have to pass for one set of changing constants, and the paper reports three of them. The check most likely to settle the argument, a cosmic microwave background calculation, is described in the report as work not yet published. That gap is the story.

What the model puts in place of the invisible

CCC+TL stands for covarying coupling constants plus tired light. The model takes dark matter and dark energy out of the equations. Their observed effects are attributed to fundamental constants that evolve over cosmic time, the quantities that govern how forces and particles interact. Tired light carries the dimming of distant supernovae that expansion usually explains.

That swap is not cosmetic. Changing the coupling constants means the Einstein and Friedmann equations no longer do what the standard model needs. The team says it wrote CCC+TL-specific equations and code for each test. One calculation was not bent to fit several problems.

Nucleosynthesis is the first gate

The early universe sets a hard floor for any cosmology. In the first minutes after the Big Bang, protons and neutrons fused into hydrogen, helium and the lighter elements. Those ratios are measurable today in old gas. A model that changes gravity, expansion or coupling strengths has to reproduce them.

According to the report, the predicted abundances of hydrogen, helium and the other light elements match what the standard model gets right. That is the point of the paper's title. The test is a consistency test, so the result is agreement rather than a fresh measurement.

The distinction limits how much credit the result deserves. Clearing a check you were built to clear is weaker evidence than clearing one you were not. The paper is careful about which of the two it is reporting.

175 rotation curves and no halo

The rotation-curve work was reported in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. Its scope is the hardest part of the case to wave away. The authors reproduced the rotation curves of all 175 galaxies in the SPARC database. No dark matter halo was added to any of them.

Gupta's line in the report is a claim about breadth, not depth. "Most emerging models explain only a limited set of observations," he says. The SPARC result is his evidence: one gravitational recipe, 175 galaxies, no per-galaxy tuning recorded in the summary.

The age arithmetic

The constants are free to change and the invisible sector is gone. The model's age for the universe then comes out at 26.7 billion years, which the report calls nearly twice what is currently believed. An older universe follows from a different expansion history. It shows up wherever age is measured.

The independent check the report cites is cosmic chronometers: passively evolving galaxies whose differential ages act as clocks. CCC+TL ages agree with those measurements, according to the authors. Two clocks reading the same hour is reassuring. Neither of them is the clock most cosmologists would ask to see.

Early galaxies stop being an emergency

Deep fields found massive galaxies at redshifts where the standard model says there was not enough time to build them. That tension has grown louder as JWST added to the list. The CCC+TL answer is to add time before the first galaxies formed. At the same observed redshift, cosmic dawn would be about 4.6 billion years old. The standard model allows roughly 300 million.

With that much more time, early galaxies come out roughly 12 times larger in size and mass than standard-model predictions. That result was reported in the journal Galaxies. "Impossible early galaxies become predictable galaxies," Gupta says. It is the strongest selling point in the material, and the one a reader should weigh against the model's cost elsewhere.

The Bullet Cluster is the hardest case

The standard model's favorite evidence is a collision. In the Bullet Cluster, hot gas and gravitational lensing separated. The extra gravity followed the galaxies instead of the gas that holds most of the ordinary mass. A modified-gravity model has to explain that offset without inventing something invisible.

The report says CCC+TL's extra gravitational effect tracks the galaxies rather than the hot X-ray gas. The language around that result is cautious: an early proof of concept that needs more study. The paper does not yet carry a full re-analysis of the cluster.

What has not been tested

The CMB test is unpublished.

The cosmic microwave background is where the standard model carries most of its evidence. A cosmology that removes dark energy has to face that pattern of peaks. It has to explain why an older universe reproduces them, or accept that it does not. The report describes a challenge of that kind as work not yet published.

A separate study supporting the model on supernova time dilation is, by the same account, still under review. The article carrying these results quotes no researcher outside the team. Independent challenge is missing from the record, and a reader inherits the authors' judgment on every number above.

How to read this without overreading it

Treat the numbers in the report as the authors' own inventory. Go to the primary document, because that inventory is short enough to read in one sitting. The DOI above resolves to a consistency test with a defined scope. The rotation-curve result lives in a different journal from the early-galaxy work.

Then ask one narrow question of the next version of this story. Has the microwave background calculation appeared, and does it use the same parameter values that nucleosynthesis and the chronometers required? If it arrives and matches, a universe without dark energy becomes a live competitor rather than a well-argued exception. If it arrives and does not, the 26.7 billion years go back to being a number in one journal.
